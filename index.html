Everyone else made you pick.

Anonymous apps give you freedom to speak and no reason to. Named apps give you the reason and no freedom. Veil keeps the relationship and removes the exposure.

Anonymous today

Fills with strangers. Anyone can write to anyone, so most of what arrives is noise — and the moment a sender sees a “12 confessions received” counter, they optimise for cruelty instead of honesty.

Freedom to speak. No reason to.

Named today

Solves the reason by solving the exposure. A handle is still a consistent distinguishing mark, and in a campus of 30,000 with a dense graph, “you recognise that gradient” is all anyone needs.

The reason. No freedom.

Five moves. That’s it.

No swiping, no discovery feed, no matches to browse. You can only write to someone a verified connection already exists with.

  1. 1

    Verify you’re a student

    18+ and a .edu address. No K‑12, no exceptions — the age floor is a legal constraint, not a content setting.

  2. 2

    Build your circle

    People you already have a verified connection to, suggested by mutual degree rather than looks. Three channels are required before you can write a word.

  3. 3

    Write it

    To one person or a 5–8 person Circle Drop. Draft-first, with a pre-send tone pass. A phone number is a hard block; everything else is advice.

  4. 4

    It arrives quietly

    Cover traffic, 10–90 minute jitter, and cohort floors, so arrival time reveals nothing about send time. Unread after 48 hours and it dissolves.

  5. 5

    They sign it back

    Either side can put their name to it. Only voluntarily, only mutually, never silently, never to anyone else. Forever optional, both ways.

Six things this will never do

Not “not in v1.” Not “behind a flag.” Each one would need a founder-level reversal.

  • Nocold DMs. A channel has to exist before you can write. One rule removes most abuse before moderation is involved.
  • Noavatars. Not a silhouette, not initials, not a generated gradient. Anonymity is enforced by the absence of a distinguishing mark, so the absence has to be architectural.
  • Nocounts. No reply counts, no follower counts, no view counts. Counts turn people into metrics.
  • Nopublic feed. The most requested and most dangerous feature. A graph product with a feed is a broadcast product, and broadcast is the opposite of the promise.
  • Nophotos or voice. In a small graph a photograph is an identity. Fixing that needs a new anonymity design, not an upload button.
  • Nopaid visibility. A purchased impression is a lie about a relationship. No ads in the content surface, ever.
The moment

Sign it

The identity that “knows” you is never the person you’re talking to. So the payoff isn’t anonymity — it’s the moment someone chooses to put their name to what they said to you, and you find out it was the person you hoped.

  • Mutual. You’re asked separately, and their answer is never shown to you.
  • Voluntary. Signing is never a condition of anything and never expires as an option.
  • Never silent. Nobody can be revealed without their own action. There is no server-side unmasking path.

No one else on campus sees either of your names. Not even moderators.

A promise you can actually check

A slogan nobody can verify is a slogan. These are the mechanisms, and the numbers are floors rather than targets.

cover traffic — an empty inbox is indistinguishable from a busy one, so volume carries no signal.
10–90 min jitter — arrival time can’t be back-solved into send time.
cohort floor 250 — no campus can open a graph small enough to read.
k ≥ 25 — no recipient is ever 1-of-N, so no single reader is an outlier.
48h dissolve — nothing waits forever to be opened, so nothing is leverage.
no read receipts — you can’t learn whether one specific person read you.

The honest risk

In a small graph, a determined person may work out who sent something. That can’t be engineered away, and any product claiming otherwise is lying. What we can do is make the promise precisely true, make inference expensive and noisy, and say the limit plainly in the product itself. An app that overclaims here is worse than one that admits the limit.
